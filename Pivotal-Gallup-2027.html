Where the Work on Women’s Health and Power Goes Next

The Gallup-Pivotal Women’s Health Survey established the connection at a national scale, across a wide range of topics. It also surfaced the questions a broad study can raise but not answer. These directions follow those questions in depth, into the places the data pointed.

What follows is a set of directions rather than a fixed scope. Open any theme to see the project ideas within it, then open a pathway to see an illustrative shape, how it would run, and a planning-level investment range. Every direction is designed to be scoped collaboratively with the Pivotal team.

The Foundation

What the Survey Surfaced

The Gallup-Pivotal Women’s Health Survey quantified how health shapes the decisions women make about work, family, and public life. It gave the case for investing in women’s health something it had been missing: national evidence, drawn from a probability sample, that connects a health experience to a life consequence.

That study was built for breadth. It covered a wide set of topics to establish the connection broadly, and in doing so it pointed clearly to where a closer look would pay off. The directions here take those signals and go in depth, one subject at a time, far enough to produce findings that stand on their own and give partners something new to act on.

Areas for deeper research
  • Workplace impacts, from how health reshapes careers to what employers can change
  • The provider experience, including disparities by race and ethnicity
  • Mental health, across life stages and generations
  • Menopause and the midlife working years
Building on the first study

The Gallup-Pivotal Women’s Health Survey already gave Pivotal the evidence and the platform. Its findings put national numbers to what had too often been treated as anecdote:

51%
of women report at least one negative experience with a healthcare provider in the past five years, compared with 39% of men
42%
say their health has negatively affected their work or career in at least one way in the past five years
74%
of women aged 45 to 54 report current or past symptoms of menopause or perimenopause

The response to those findings is the case for continuing now. Conversations the study has prompted with media point to clear appetite for deeper reporting on women’s health. The data also holds more than a single release could carry: some findings, including on mental health, were held for timing, a sign both that there is more to say and that how a program is sequenced shapes how far it travels. Building on that momentum, rather than letting it settle, is what turns a strong debut into a lasting position and establishes Pivotal as the definitive voice on women’s health.

Four Directions to Go Deeper

Choose the Depth, Not Just the Topic

Each theme carries more than one way to pursue it. A single focused study, an ongoing measurement program, a quantitative deep dive, or a quantitative study paired with the voices behind the numbers. Open a theme to see its pathways.

1 Primary recommendation Women, Health, and Power at Work The clearest throughline in Pivotal’s agenda, and the place the survey findings pointed most directly. Go inside the workplace to explain how health reshapes women’s trajectories, and what employers can change.

The survey showed that health causes women to step back. Going deeper asks the harder questions: where in a career it happens, why, and what makes the difference between a woman who stays and advances and one who quietly disengages. The workplace is where women’s economic power is won or lost, and it is where the evidence can speak most directly to employers, benefits designers, and policymakers. These pathways introduce constructs the flagship survey did not reach, including the hidden work of concealing a condition, the gap between benefits offered and benefits usable, productivity eroded while still on the job, and the role of the direct manager in whether health becomes a career setback.

Two pathways
1A Standalone Workplace & Health Study A dedicated survey of working women, sized for deep workforce cuts $300K–$450KILLUSTRATIVE

Illustrative scope

  • Dedicated survey of working U.S. women via the probability-based Gallup Panel, sized for reliable reporting by industry, occupation and job family, seniority, and life stage
  • A questionnaire built around the workplace itself: perceived bias and differential treatment, disclosure and concealment of health conditions, awareness and use of accommodations, and confidence to seek advancement
  • New constructs the first survey did not reach, including presenteeism (capability lost while present, not only days missed) and the manager relationship as a buffer or barrier
  • Pivotal typing tool questions carried through so the data segments to Pivotal’s audiences at analysis
  • Optional working-men reference points on select measures where a contrast sharpens the story

Concepts worth reaching for

  • Lifecycle leak points: mapping health-related step-backs to specific career moments, from early career to the perimenopausal midlife and the caregiving squeeze
  • The concealment tax: what women do not disclose to managers or HR, and what they fear happens if they do
  • The benefits reality gap: the distance between benefits on paper and benefits women can actually use
  • A health-safety climate: whether a workplace feels safe enough to raise a health need at all

1B Workforce Tracking Integration Build on measurement and reporting Gallup already produces on women at work $175K–$300KPER YEAR, ILLUSTRATIVE

Illustrative scope

  • Women at work is an established, ongoing subject of Gallup research, not new ground; this pathway lets Pivotal shape and amplify work already in the field
  • Add up to ten Pivotal-defined items to Gallup’s quarterly measurement of more than 20,000 employed U.S. adults, sized for deep worker-type cuts by job family and level, industry, and sector
  • Women’s health items sit alongside outcomes Gallup already tracks, so health can be read against engagement, wellbeing, burnout, and intent to leave
  • Or co-sponsor the reporting Gallup already produces on women in the workplace, establishing it as a consistent, named topic rather than an occasional one

Why this pathway is distinct

  • It builds on expertise and data Gallup has developed over time, so Pivotal amplifies a proven line of work rather than starting one
  • Triangulation is the payoff: a women’s health signal gains weight when it moves in step with engagement or burnout across tens of thousands of workers
  • Sample depth makes narrow, defensible subgroup claims possible that a one-time survey cannot support
  • It keeps women’s workplace health in steady public view between larger studies

Reporting already underway

2 Inside the Provider Experience The survey asked whether care was delayed or dismissed. This goes inside the clinical encounter itself, from booking the appointment to the follow-up, to document where women’s experiences diverge from men’s.

A woman’s health outcome is shaped long before a diagnosis, in how she is scheduled, greeted, questioned, believed, and advised. This theme maps the full journey of care and looks hard at where it treats women, and women of color in particular, differently. A deliberate oversample makes it possible to report on women of color on their own terms rather than as a footnote to a national average.

Two pathways
2A Provider Experience Deep Dive A journey-based survey with an oversample of women of color $325K–$475KILLUSTRATIVE

Illustrative scope

  • Representative survey of U.S. women via the Gallup Panel, with a planned oversample of women of color for reliable subgroup reporting
  • A journey-based instrument tracing the full encounter: scheduling, front-desk and staff interaction, clinician communication, being believed rather than dismissed, the guidance received, and follow-up
  • Cuts by care setting (emergency, primary, specialty), visit type, frequency of care, and provider type
  • Optional men’s reference points on select measures of being believed or dismissed, where the contrast is the finding

What it produces

  • A defensible account of where the care experience diverges by gender and by race
  • Subgroup findings on women of color that stand on their own
  • Evidence tied to specific, changeable points in the encounter, not a general sense of dissatisfaction

How it operates

01 Journey-mapping workshop 02 Instrument design with oversample plan 03 Fielding & weighting 04 Subgroup analysis 05 Designed report
$325K–$475K illustrative planning range Oversampling drives much of the range, along with instrument length and reporting depth. Figures are illustrative and for planning conversation only.
2B Deep Dive Plus Qualitative Recontact The survey above, plus in-depth interviews and quotes in the report $425K–$625KILLUSTRATIVE

Illustrative scope

3 Mental Health, in Depth Take the mental-health thread the survey opened and give it room, with the option to center young women and pair the numbers with lived experience.

Mental health ran through the survey findings without being the subject of them. This theme makes it the subject: how women experience mental health, how diagnoses and symptom burden differ across life stages, where care is sought and where it stalls, and how a younger generation is navigating it differently. The theme can travel light as a module on a larger survey, or stand alone with the sample depth to speak to young women specifically.

4 Menopause and the Midlife Workplace A focused study of women in midlife, built to trace how symptoms women may not name, and rarely disclose, reshape their working lives.

Menopause arrives in the middle of a career, often unnamed and rarely discussed at work, and its effect on women’s trajectories is almost entirely undocumented at scale. This theme concentrates on women aged 35 to 55 and works backward from age into experience: how women attribute symptoms, whether and to whom they disclose, and how all of it lands on their working lives. A front-loaded desk review sharpens the constructs before a single question is written.

Two pathways
5A Focused Midlife Study Women 35 to 55, with a desk-research foundation $225K–$375KILLUSTRATIVE

Illustrative scope

  • Representative survey of U.S. women aged 35 to 55 via the Gallup Panel
  • A front-loaded desk review to sharpen the constructs: how women attribute symptoms to perimenopause and menopause, and how often those symptoms go unnamed
  • Disclosure behavior at work, awareness and use of accommodations, and the career decisions that follow
  • Retrospective questions that connect symptom onset to specific workplace choices

What it produces

  • A first clear read on how midlife symptoms shape women’s working lives
  • Evidence an employer or benefits designer can act on
  • A finding that connects a stigmatized, private experience to public, economic consequence

How it operates

How the Program Comes Together

From Directions to a Defined Program

These directions are meant to be discussed, combined, and shaped together. Several can be sequenced across the year, or a single flagship can carry more than one theme through shared modules and oversamples.

First

Choose the directions

We talk through which themes matter most and which pathway fits each one, with workplace as the recommended anchor.

Then

Shape the program

We scope the selected work into a defined plan: instruments, samples, oversamples, cadence, and the reporting and communications that carry it.

Throughout

Keep the drumbeat

We design each release for coverage and each finding for reuse, so the research reads as a sustained body of work rather than a series of one-off studies.

Communications and Activation

The Work Continues After the Findings Land

Communications support is part of the work throughout, not a step at the end. Each study is designed for coverage from the first question, and a dedicated effort carries every finding into the conversations that give it reach. The same amplification that shaped the first survey continues, drawing on all the studies in the program and sustaining momentum in the stretches between releases.

Media strategy and rollout. Embargoed release planning, press outreach, and coordinated timing so each finding arrives where it will be seen.
Bylines and commentary. Op-eds and authored pieces that put the evidence in front of the audiences Pivotal wants to move.
Briefings and convenings. Roundtables and closed briefings that gather employers, funders, and policymakers around a result.
Social and digital content. Content produced for Pivotal’s channels and for Gallup’s, translating findings into shareable, durable assets.
Ongoing amplification. Keeping findings in circulation across the year, so a study keeps working long after its release week.
A connected narrative. Tying each release back to the first survey and forward to what comes next, so the body of work reads as one sustained argument.

Scope and cadence for communications and activation are shaped alongside the research, so the effort matches the studies chosen and the moments that matter most to Pivotal across the year.
