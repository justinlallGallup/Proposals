Overview

Two studies, built to work together

On July 14, 2026, the 61st anniversary of the Older Americans Act, the Senate passed S. 2120 by unanimous consent, and the bill now awaits action in the House. Whether reauthorization moves in this session or carries into the 120th Congress, the Foundation for Social Connection and its Action Network will be making a specific case to Congress: that the OAA is the primary federal policy supporting social connection for older adults and the people who care for them.

The scientific side of that case is well developed, much of it advanced by F4SC and its Scientific Leadership Council. The piece F4SC has identified as missing is the public opinion record: whether Americans know these programs exist, whether they connect them to social connection, which arguments persuade, and whether support holds across party lines. That record is what congressional offices, and the partner organizations in F4SC’s aging strategy group, will want to see.

Gallup proposes two coordinated studies. Part 1 is confidential message testing for F4SC’s strategy. Part 2 is a public study, designed with F4SC and published by Gallup, that any advocate, journalist or policymaker can cite. Both draw on the Gallup Panel, the largest probability-based research panel in the U.S., and on Gallup’s research on social connection and wellbeing. Either can be commissioned on its own, and the two can share a single survey or run in sequence.

Two studies, two sets of rules

Gallup holds public research and internal strategy research to different standards. When both parts share one survey, the public items come first, before any respondent sees a test message, and only those items are published. That lets Part 1 test advocacy language freely while Part 2 stays neutral enough to be cited by anyone.

Part 1: Message testing

Which messages move voters, and which voters they move

Confidential testing that tells F4SC which arguments for social connection programs resonate most strongly, overall and with the audiences that matter most in congressional conversations.

The seven territories come from F4SC’s brief. Final messages are drafted with F4SC, and Gallup’s methodologists refine the wording so each message tests one idea at a consistent length and tone, which keeps results about the idea rather than the phrasing.

How the test works

  1. Benchmark first

    Before seeing any message, respondents say whether social isolation among older adults is an issue and how they rank it as a priority. Every message is measured against this baseline.

  2. Rate each message

    Each respondent rates a randomized set drawn from a pool of 10 to 20 messages on how convincing it is and how much it motivates them to care about the issue.

  3. Force the trade-off

    Head-to-head choices between messages (a MaxDiff design) produce a true rank order, separating the message that wins from messages that simply score well.

  4. Read by audience

    Results by party, age (including adults 40+ and the sandwich generation), geography including rural communities, and caregivers, showing which messages unite and which divide.

Measuring persuasion, if useful. Where F4SC wants to know how far a message moves opinion, a control group that sees no messages gives a clean comparison for priority and support.

What F4SC receives

A messaging roadmap identifying the strongest framing and policy arguments for continued public education and policy advocacy, the outcome named in F4SC’s brief.

  • Messages ranked overall and for each priority audience
  • The frames that hold across Republicans, Democrats and independents, and the ones that split them
  • Language to lead with, and language to avoid
  • A working session with Gallup’s team to translate results into F4SC’s materials
Design notes for Part 1

Part 1 is priced for n=3,000 U.S. adults from the Gallup Panel and an instrument of up to 5 minutes: nationally representative, with adults 40 and older oversampled within the total. Registered voters are identified so results can be read among voters as well as all adults. Within a 5-minute instrument, each respondent rates a subset of the message pool, so every message is still rated by a large, representative group. The number of messages per respondent is set in design.

Gallup also conducts focus groups, which can add directional insight for internal strategy before or after the survey. They can be scoped alongside Part 1 on request.

Part 2: Public study

What Americans know, and what they support

A nationally representative Gallup study of awareness, concern and support, written in Gallup’s own voice and built to be cited by advocates, journalists and policymakers.

F4SC’s questionHow Gallup would measure it
What is Americans’ baseline level of social connectedness, and what does it look like for older adults?Established Gallup measures of connection, isolation and loneliness, kept distinct in line with F4SC’s definitions and reported for older adults and every other age group.
How concerned are Americans about isolation among older adults, and how much do they know about the solutions in place?Balanced items on concern and on awareness of solutions, with no framing that signals a preferred answer.
How much do Americans know about the OAA and the programs it supports?Awareness of the law by name, measured separately from awareness and use of the programs it funds, such as senior centers, congregate and home-delivered meals, transportation and family caregiver support. People can know a program well without knowing what funds it, so the two are kept apart.
Do older adults and caregivers know how to find connection opportunities in their communities?Knowledge of local options and where to turn, among adults 60 and older (the OAA’s core eligible population) and family caregivers.
Is support for older adults and social connection shared across the electorate?Neutral measures of the priority Americans place on a federal role in supporting older adults, reported by party, age and other groups, among all adults and among registered voters.

Precision matters here. In Gallup’s global research with Meta, adults 65 and older reported the lowest loneliness of any age group. That is exactly why the instrument should measure isolation, loneliness and access to connection as separate things, as F4SC’s own definitions do. A study that blurs them invites the wrong headline.

From research questions to public release

  1. Discovery

    Review of the literature, Gallup’s existing social connection and wellbeing items, and F4SC’s research questions.

  2. Draft instrument

    Gallup methodologists draft the questionnaire for F4SC’s review. An existing F4SC instrument can be tested instead.

  3. Cognitive testing

    Items are tested with respondents to confirm they mean what they are meant to mean.

  4. Release review

    Gallup’s Public Release Committee reviews the design against Gallup’s standards for public research.

  5. Fielding

    The survey goes to a national sample from the Gallup Panel, with the planned oversample.

  6. Weighting and analysis

    Results are weighted to national benchmarks and analyzed for every reporting group.

  7. Reporting

    Gallup writes the report: what the data say, in charts and plain language, reviewed before release.

  8. Public release

    Findings publish under Gallup’s name, with launch support scaled to the option F4SC chooses.

Optional

In their own words: follow-up interviews

After the survey closes, Gallup can recontact consenting respondents, such as older adults, caregivers and people who use OAA programs, for in-depth interviews about their experiences. Quotes are reported in the aggregate inside the public report, giving the numbers a human voice. For public research, Gallup recommends in-depth interviews over focus groups because they yield fuller, quotable accounts.

Gallup used this approach in United States of Scams, its 2026 study with the Stop Scams Alliance, which paired national estimates with a section of scam victims’ accounts in their own words.

Why Gallup

Research built to be cited, from the first question

Public opinion data helps a policy case only if the people hearing it believe it. Gallup’s public research rests on a probability-based sample, a review process that keeps every published study neutral, and more than nine decades of measuring what Americans think. For public release, a probability-based sample is the standard newsrooms, policymakers and researchers look for.

100,000+

U.S. adults in the Gallup Panel, recruited at random through address-based sampling and random-digit dialing. No one can opt in, so results avoid the self-selection that affects opt-in online samples, and they can be projected to the national population and its subgroups.

142

Countries in Gallup and Meta’s Global State of Social Connections, alongside Gallup’s ongoing U.S. measurement of loneliness, social wellbeing and time with family and friends. Part 2 can draw on that item bank from the start.

1935

The year Gallup began measuring American public opinion. Today every public-facing Gallup study is reviewed by Gallup’s Public Release Committee, so the findings F4SC cites carry Gallup’s standards into every hearing room and newsroom.

F4SC brings the science and the coalition. This research adds the public record.
