The Opportunity

A population measure for small businesses, built on a proven model

The BBB Institute for Marketplace Trust tracks scams against small businesses through BBB Scam Tracker and its annual Risk Report, and it has partnered with the Federal Trade Commission on earlier small business research. That work has shown the problem is real and widespread: in 2024, about 29 percent of small businesses that were targeted reported losing money. What reported data captures, by its nature, is the businesses that come forward, and small businesses have particular reasons to stay quiet, from reputational concerns to worry about future business. A measure built on reports can understate how widely scams reach.

Gallup's Stop Scams Alliance study showed what a nationally representative survey adds. By measuring U.S. adults directly, rather than only those who report, it found that consumers lost an estimated 68 billion dollars to scams in 2025, a figure far larger than reported totals, and that only 13 percent of scams ever reached the Federal Trade Commission or federal law enforcement. The same approach, dedicated to small businesses, would reach owners whether or not they have ever filed a report, and would sit alongside the Risk Report as a complementary, population-level measure.

The timing matters too. Scam tactics are changing quickly, and AI and deepfakes already featured in an estimated 12 percent of successful scams against consumers in 2025. A dedicated small business survey, refreshed on a regular cadence, would let the Institute see how exposure is shifting and point its education where the need is moving.

Within weeks of release, the Stop Scams Alliance study became the reference point for consumer scams. A study built the same way, dedicated to small businesses, could do the same, and give the Institute a measure it can refresh over time.

Gallup would build this as a new, dedicated small business study, developed with the Institute and modeled on the Stop Scams Alliance work. BBB is looking to expand its fraud prevention and education, and the study is built to serve exactly that: findings that deepen the Institute's understanding of the challenges small businesses face and inform future programming, read by industry, by business size, and by threat type so resources reach the owners and sectors where the risk is greatest.

Our Approach

How Gallup would build the measure

The heart of the work is the method. The study is built the way Gallup builds its longest-running measures: define the agenda together, develop and test the instrument carefully, field it to a representative sample, and refine it each year so the core metrics can be trended with confidence. The Institute's research team would be at the table from the first phase.

1Phase I

Research agenda and foundation

Gallup begins by working with the Institute's research team, and invited experts where useful, to prioritize the constructs that matter most. This phase draws on the Institute's Scam Tracker and Risk Report experience, on the instrument Gallup developed for the Stop Scams Alliance, and on Gallup's own archive of validated items and long-running trends on trust in institutions. Where it helps anchor the agenda, Gallup conducts structured stakeholder interviews and a focused review of existing research before any questions are written.

2Phase I

Survey instrument development

Gallup translates the agreed priorities into a survey instrument through an iterative process: Gallup drafts, the Institute reviews, and the two refine together, typically across at least two rounds. This is deliberate work. Measuring something people are reluctant to disclose depends on how a question is framed, so the wording, order, and response options are developed with care rather than assumed. The specific areas the instrument covers are settled here, with the Institute, and may include how businesses are targeted and what a scam costs them, cybersecurity and protective readiness, where a scam enters day-to-day operations, and the effect on owners and staff.

3Phase I

Cognitive interviews

Before fielding, Gallup conducts cognitive interviews with small business owners to confirm that each item is clear, interpreted as intended, and answerable without undue burden. Participants walk through how they read each question and where wording could be sharper. Their feedback refines the instrument and reduces the chance of ambiguous or unreliable data once the survey is live.

4Phase II

Sampling and data collection

Gallup fields the survey to a nationally representative sample of U.S. small business owners and senior decision-makers, using its probability-based Gallup Panel supplemented with a vetted partner sample to reach the full range of businesses by size, industry, and region. Unlike opt-in panels, Gallup Panel members are recruited through scientifically randomized methods and are not self-selected, which is what allows the results to represent the population rather than only those who choose to respond. Gallup applies a minimum reporting threshold of 300 completed surveys in any category before reporting it publicly.

5Phase II

Analysis

Gallup weights the data to correct for nonresponse and projects it to known benchmarks for the U.S. small business population, then analyzes it through descriptive statistics, crosstabulations, and, where the questions call for it, multivariate modeling. The analysis is built to support the reads the Institute needs most: by industry, by business size, and by threat type.

6Phase III

Reporting and amplification

Gallup works with the Institute to identify the findings that matter most and produces a report built for both credibility and reach, which can live on a co-branded research hub on Gallup.com. Gallup's in-house strategic communications team designs the release for reach from the start, through targeted media outreach, coordinated messaging with the Institute, and, where useful, briefings for the practitioners and policymakers positioned to act on the findings.

7Each year

Annual evolution

Each year, Gallup reviews and refines the instrument, holding the core measures steady for year-over-year comparison while rotating in timely items that reflect new tactics in the marketplace. In the early years, Gallup can test wording and structure so the core metrics are rigorously established before they are trended. The result is a durable benchmark the Institute can return to, not a one-time snapshot.

Why Gallup

A measure of something people are reluctant to admit carries weight only if the public believes the number. That is what Gallup brings.

Gallup has spent decades measuring what institutions Americans trust, building the probability-based methods that make a population estimate credible, and getting findings in front of the audiences who act on them. The same strengths apply directly here.

50+ yrs

Authority in measuring trust

Gallup has tracked Americans' confidence in institutions for more than half a century. That depth is what lets a new measure be placed in context rather than read in isolation.

Probability

A representative panel

The Gallup Panel is recruited through scientifically randomized methods, not opt-in sign-ups. It reaches groups often missed in business research, including the smallest firms, and supports estimates that represent the population.

140K+

Reach that moves findings

Gallup's news service earns more than 140,000 media mentions a year across 100-plus countries, and publishes to Associated Press standards. Findings reach the press, policymakers, and the public, not just the field.

Independent

No stake in the result

Gallup carries no position on the outcome. The findings stand on method alone, which is what lets leaders and the business community act on them with confidence.

Relevant Work

A track record with small businesses, and with scams

Three current and recent Gallup programs show the pieces this study brings together: a coalition-funded measure of business owners, a long-running small business index, and the consumer study this work would extend.

JPMorganChase & Kauffman Foundation

Pathways to Wealth

A multi-year Gallup initiative, funded by JPMorganChase and the Ewing Marion Kauffman Foundation, on how business ownership connects to income, wealth, and wellbeing. Its Year 3 survey reached nearly 6,000 working adults, including more than 3,500 business owners, drawn primarily from the probability-based Gallup Panel, with a co-branded research hub on Gallup.com.

Stop Scams Alliance

United States of Scams

Gallup's 2026 study with the Stop Scams Alliance produced a nationally representative measure of how scams affect U.S. adults, surveying more than 5,100 Americans via the Gallup Panel. It found consumers lost an estimated $68 billion in 2025, was provided exclusively to the Associated Press, and was picked up across national media within days. This study would extend that model to small businesses.
