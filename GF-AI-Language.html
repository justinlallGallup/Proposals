A partnership proposal from Gallup

Measuring who can use AI in their own language and voice

The coalition's goal is to bring useful AI to an estimated 3.4 billion people who speak languages today's models serve poorly. To steer that work and show it is succeeding, the coalition needs to measure it. Today it cannot, because the data do not exist.

What exists is the supply side: which languages the models support. What is missing is the demand side, the people's side. No one has measured, at scale, how many people need AI in a language it does not yet serve. And no one has measured how many are getting by in a language they are not comfortable in, who would use AI more, and for more, if it worked in the language they know best.

Gallup proposes to build that missing measure, in three steps. First, establish the demand: an objective, official baseline of need. Second, baseline the current state: how many people can already use AI in the language they prefer. Third, track both over time, so progress toward the goal can be seen. This is a measurement platform, not a one-time study.

There is a direct precedent. Before 2011, financial inclusion was measured only from the supply side, by counting bank branches and accounts. With funding from the Gates Foundation, the World Bank and Gallup built the Global Findex, the demand-side measure that asked people directly whether they use an account, and why not. It became the world's reference statistic for inclusion. Language access can follow the same path, on the same survey.

The recommendation is to start now: add a short, carefully tested set of items to the 2027 Gallup World Poll, the annual, nationally representative survey already fielded in more than 140 countries and 145 languages, and already carrying Gallup's AI research with Microsoft. Those items establish the first global baseline of language need and current access. In 2028, a fuller module and focused study of priority language communities can follow.

1 The gap we would close We can see which languages the models support. No one has measured, from the people's side, who needs AI in a language it does not serve, or who is getting by in one they are not comfortable in. +

The supply side is visible. Coverage benchmarks, provider data, and diffusion reports show which languages the models handle and where AI is used. They answer one question: what do the tools support.

The demand side is not. It answers a different question, the one the coalition's goal depends on: what do people actually need, and use. Two blanks matter most.

The unmet need

How many people would use AI for health, money, government, farming, or their work if it worked in the language they are most comfortable in, and cannot today. This is the objective baseline of need, and it does not exist.

The value left unrealized

How many people already use AI in a second language they are not fully comfortable in, getting less from it than they would in their own. This is latent demand that better language support would unlock, and it is invisible in any supply-side source.

Native-speaker counts cannot answer either. A first-language count is a unique number, not a measure of need: a person may already be served through a second language, or may prefer their own language for a health or financial decision. Only asking people directly, the same way everywhere, resolves it. That is the measure Gallup proposes to establish.

2 The precedent: the Global Findex Gallup and the World Bank built exactly this kind of demand-side measure for financial inclusion. The Gates Foundation funded it. It became the world's reference statistic. +

Before 2011, financial inclusion was measured only from the supply side. The IMF's Financial Access Survey counted bank branches, ATMs, and accounts per capita. That showed what institutions offered, but not who was actually included, who was excluded, or why.

In 2011, with multi-year funding from the Gates Foundation, the World Bank and Gallup built the Global Findex: the first demand-side, user-side measure of financial inclusion. It asked about 150,000 adults across nearly 150 economies, directly, whether they have and use an account, how they save, borrow, and pay, and, for those without one, why not.

Because it asked people, it could do what supply data never could: disaggregate by gender, income, education, and urban or rural residence, and name who was excluded and the barriers they faced. It has been repeated roughly every three years since, so progress is visible over time. The 2025 edition, carried out on the World Poll with about 145,000 adults in 141 economies, added a Digital Connectivity Tracker linking phone and internet use to inclusion.

The Findex became the definitive global statistic for financial inclusion and a reference point for development goals worldwide. Language access is the same shape of problem: supply-side visibility, demand-side blindness. The same platform, the same partner, and the same method can close it.

3 What we would measure, starting in 2027 A short set of items on the 2027 World Poll begins the baseline across 140+ countries, and connects to everything the survey already measures, with additional areas to explore as the work develops. +

The baseline begins with a small, high-value set of items, developed and cognitively tested, added to the 2027 World Poll. They are built to separate real exclusion from ordinary choice, and sit alongside Gallup's existing AI items, so a single interview links language need to AI awareness, use, income, work, and wellbeing.

Illustrative items to begin tracking

The items below are illustrative, meant to show the kind of measures the module would carry. Final topics and exact wording would be designed in collaboration with the Gates Foundation and refined through cognitive testing across cultures before fielding.

A. Establish the demand
  • What language are you most comfortable using when getting information or help?
  • Can you get digital services and tools to work well in that language today?
B. Baseline current access
  • When you last used an AI tool, which language did you use?
  • Was that the language you are most comfortable in, or one you used because it works better?
C. Latent demand and voice
  • Would you use AI more, or for more things, if it worked well in your most comfortable language?
  • Would you rather speak or type when using these tools?

4 The platform: the Gallup World Poll The annual, nationally representative survey in 140+ countries and 145+ languages, and the one behind the Findex and the World Happiness Report. +

The Gallup World Poll is a continuous, nationally representative survey fielded every year in more than 140 countries. Every wave carries a fixed core of questions asked of everyone, everywhere. A partner adds a focused module to that core, and because it sits on the same interview, it can be analyzed against everything else the survey already collects, with no additional fielding.

What pairing with those measures unlocks

Because the language items would ride alongside these measures, a single interview can answer questions no supply-side source can.

Does language access track with higher wellbeing?
Relate the measure to the core Life Evaluation Index and the Positive and Negative Experience indices on the same wave.
Is it linked to economic confidence?
Read access against feelings about household income, standard of living, and whether people see a healthy local job market.
Who is left behind, and where?
Break access down by gender, education, and urban or rural residence, with reliable cuts from a roughly 1,000-person national sample.
Does access reach the services that matter?
Connect use for health, financial, and government tasks to core items such as quality healthcare and confidence in financial institutions.
Is access forced or chosen?
Separate people using a second language by preference from those switching because the tool fails in their own, the clearest signal of exclusion.
What changes as access grows?
Track the measure across waves alongside the same outcomes. This reveals relationships, not causation; isolating impact would need a dedicated design.
Building on work already underway

Gallup is not starting from zero on AI. On the 2026 World Poll, Gallup already measures public engagement with AI across more than 140 countries, and has done the methodological work of defining AI for respondents through concrete use cases, so the questions hold up where adoption is still new. The language and voice items would extend that work, asking not only whether a person uses AI, but whether they can use it in the language they prefer.

What the baseline delivers, and where a focused study goes deeper
Delivered directly on the World Poll
  • Nationally representative, comparable, person-level estimates of language need and current access, for covered countries, tracked over time
  • Estimates for language groups large enough within the national samples, with standard cuts including gender
  • Language access analyzed against income, work, wellbeing, and connectivity already on the same wave
  • A direct measure of preference, so chosen use is separated from forced switching rather than inferred
Where a focused study goes deeper
  • Small language communities, including languages with around a million speakers, read in depth through a focused study built around them, since they can be a small share of any single national sample
  • Whether a specific tool works for a user in practice, through task-based testing that goes beyond what a survey can report
  • An all-ages count: the World Poll measures people 15 and older

The invitation

Start the baseline on the 2027 World Poll.

The coalition has named the goal. Gallup can make progress toward it measurable: an independent, demand-side read of who needs AI in their own language, who already has it, and how that changes over time. The first step is small and valuable in itself, a short set of items on the next global wave.
