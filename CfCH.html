The Center for Civic Health

A Minneapolis home for research, guidance, insights and convenings on civic health. The Center would listen to residents, measure how they are experiencing democracy against the national Democracy for All baseline, and bring what it learns back to the people working for an inclusive democracy in the city.

Prepared by Gallup, November 2026

Minneapolis has shown its civic strength. The next step is to measure its civic health.

Last winter, as federal immigration enforcement surged across the Twin Cities, residents organized to protect one another: delivering meals to neighbors they had never met, sharing information in real time and showing up for each other in public. The Foundation’s 2026 Minnesota Meeting, Of the People, named what that revealed, the civic strength of Minnesotans. This year, the Kettering Foundation named the people of the Twin Cities recipients of the 2026 Kettering Democracy Prize.

Strength in a crisis is one sign of a community’s civic health. The everyday signs are quieter: whether residents believe they can shape what happens in their city, whether they take part when they want to, whether they feel valued and respected, and whether local institutions respond to their needs. Those answers differ across Minneapolis, and they change over time.

The Center for Civic Health would give the city a way to see them clearly and act on them, building on the Foundation’s long investment in civic engagement, its support for democracy work beyond election seasons, and the listening community organizations across Minneapolis do every day.

A national study is built to describe the nation. It cannot show how democracy feels to a family that recently arrived, to a 19-year-old voting for the first time, or to a block that lived through the surge up close. That is what the Center would measure.

What the Center is

Civic health is how well a community’s democracy works for the people who live in it: whether residents feel they can shape what happens, take part when they want to, feel valued and respected, and see local institutions respond to their needs.

The Center for Civic Health would be a standing partnership between the Minneapolis Foundation and Gallup, producing four things for the city every year.

Research

An annual citywide survey benchmarked to the national Democracy for All baseline, and focused studies on the questions residents raise.

Insights

Findings reported community by community, so leaders see how different residents experience democracy, not just a city average.

Guidance

Practical, evidence-based recommendations for nonprofits, funders and public leaders working to strengthen civic life.

Convenings

Gatherings that open each year by listening to residents and close it by working through what the evidence means.

How each year works

Three steps, repeated annually. Each cycle opens and closes with residents in the room.

1 Listen Convene residents and leaders to hear what the community needs from democracy and wants to get out of it.
2 Measure Survey the city with methods built to hear voices traditional research often misses.
3 Act Reconvene to share findings and workshop strategies for a more inclusive democracy.

1 Listen

Begin with what residents want from democracy

Each cycle opens in community, not in a questionnaire. The Center convenes residents and leaders across Minneapolis to hear what they need from local democracy, what is working and what is not, and what they hope the city’s civic life can become. What the Center hears shapes what it measures.

Community listening sessions

Facilitated conversations hosted with community organizations in neighborhoods across the city and in residents’ own languages, with deliberate attention to immigrant communities and young residents.

Leader conversations

Discussions with civic, nonprofit, faith, business and public leaders about where they see civic health strengthening, and where it is under strain.

A community advisory circle

A standing group of residents and community leaders who help set the Center’s priorities, review survey questions before fielding and review findings before release.

What comes out of it. A published listening summary, and the priorities that become each year’s Minneapolis-specific survey questions.

2 Measure

A citywide survey that reaches the residents polls often miss

The Minneapolis Civic Health Survey is the Center’s core instrument: an annual survey of adult residents across the city, built in three layers and fielded with best-in-class methods so that voices traditional survey research often misses are heard, and reported in full.

Core benchmark: Minneapolis against the nation

Questions drawn word for word from the national Democracy for All Project and fielded in the same season as the national study, so Minneapolis can be read against a national baseline of more than 23,000 U.S. adults.

  • Commitment to democracy as a form of government
  • How democracy is performing, in the nation and in the city
  • How much power ordinary people have to create change
  • Whether local officials are sensitive to residents’ needs
  • Belonging: whether residents feel valued and respected
  • Participation: volunteering, community events, voting and contacting officials
  • What holds residents back, and what would move them to act
  • Neighbors and local sources of information
  • Satisfaction with housing, healthcare, schools, childcare and food
  • Financial security and the outlook for the next generation
  • Hopes for the future, including the one thing that would most improve democracy, in residents’ own words

Sample items from the national study

“Whether you agree or disagree with current political leadership, how do you think democracy is doing in your city or town?”

“How much power do ordinary people have to create change in your local community?”

“Government officials in my city/town are sensitive to the interests of people like me when making decisions.”

The city survey and the national study draw different samples, so Minneapolis results are reported against the national baseline with matched question wording and timing, and any design differences are noted alongside each comparison.

Minneapolis modules: questions written for this city

Two modules, developed with residents during Listen, go deeper on the issues shaping civic life in Minneapolis now.

Immigrant justice and civic life

Statewide, more than one in four Minnesotans say fear of judgment or retaliation keeps them from getting involved. The national study cannot show how that is distributed across Minneapolis, or how the surge changed it.Source: Kettering-Gallup Democracy for All studies, 2025 and 2026, Minnesota estimates.

Explores how federal immigration enforcement affected residents, families and neighborhoods; whether residents feel safe taking part in public life; confidence that local institutions protect residents’ rights; access to legal help and reliable information; the mutual aid networks residents built; and what immigrant residents and their neighbors want local democracy to do next.

Illustrative items

“In the past year, how much has federal immigration enforcement affected you or your family?”

“How safe do you feel taking part in public life in Minneapolis, such as attending a community meeting or contacting a city office?”

No question asks about immigration status. The module measures experience, not status.

Young Minneapolis and democracy

Nationally, 53% of adults aged 18 to 29 say democracy is the best form of government, compared with 80% of those 65 and older. Young adults report more barriers to participation than any other age group, yet nearly half of those who don’t volunteer say they have wanted to.Source: Kettering-Gallup Democracy for All Project, 2025 survey.

Explores where young residents get information and whose voices they listen to; whether anyone has invited them to take part; civic education in school and at home; belonging and isolation; their economic outlook; and what would make local decisions feel worth their time.

Illustrative items

“Has anyone ever invited you to help improve your community?”

“How much power do people your age have to change how things are done in Minneapolis?”

Young adults are oversampled so their results can be reported on their own.

Illustrative items only. Module questions are developed with community input during Listen and tested in every survey language before fielding.

Responsive questions: set by what the city raises

Each year, a block of the survey is reserved for the questions residents and leaders raise in Listen. It keeps the Center current without disturbing the trend. Topics might include:

  • Public safety and how residents experience it
  • Housing and staying in the neighborhood
  • The future of downtown
  • Local news and where residents get information

Between annual surveys, the Center can also run short pulse studies with residents who agree to be recontacted, so it can respond when events reshape civic life.

Reaching voices often unheard

Methods chosen so that every resident has a real chance to be heard, whether or not they are online, already engaged or comfortable in English.

A random sample of the whole city

Gallup draws a probability sample of Minneapolis addresses, so every household has a known chance of being invited, whether or not its residents are online or already involved in civic life.

Web and mail, in residents’ languages

Residents respond online or on paper, mirroring the national study’s design for reaching adults missed by online-only research. Materials are translated and tested, likely in Spanish, Somali and Hmong, with other languages set with community partners.

Deliberate oversamples

Additional sample where immigrant residents and young adults live, so their results stand on their own rather than disappearing into a city average.

Community partners in the field

Community organizations let residents know the survey is coming, why it matters and how their answers are protected.

Protection by design

Responses are confidential and reported only in aggregate. Nothing is released in a form that could identify a resident.

Weighted to the city

Results are weighted to Census benchmarks for Minneapolis adults, so the citywide picture reflects who actually lives there.

Illustrative design, set in scoping: 2,000 to 3,000 completed surveys a year, sized to the number of communities the Center commits to reporting on.

Minneapolis residents, not a monolith

Every finding is reported for the groups that make up the city, wherever samples allow. Where a group is too small to report reliably, the Center says so rather than guessing.

  • Community area
  • Age and generation
  • Immigrant generation
  • Race and ethnicity
  • Language
  • Income and financial security
  • Renters and homeowners
  • Experience of the surge
3 Act

Bring the evidence back to the people who can use it

Each cycle closes the way it opened, with residents and leaders in the room. The Center returns findings first to the communities that shaped them, then works with leaders to turn evidence into strategies that keep improving civic health and build a more inclusive democracy in Minneapolis.

Community findings sessions

Results shared first with the communities and partners who took part in Listen, in their languages and in their neighborhoods.

Strategy workshops

Working sessions where civic leaders, nonprofits, funders and public officials work through the findings, choose what to try, and decide what the Center should measure to know whether it worked.

Guidance for practice

Short, practical guides drawn from the evidence, such as how to reach residents who want to take part but have never been asked.

The public release

The annual State of Civic Health in Minneapolis report, released at a moment of the Foundation’s choosing, such as the Minnesota Meeting, with Gallup.com and Gallup’s media relationships extending its reach.

What comes out of it. Strategies leaders have chosen to pursue, guidance organizations can use, and the questions that open the next year’s Listen.

Year one, and every year after

The first full cycle sets the baseline. Each year after repeats the core, so Minneapolis can see its civic health change over time, alongside the multiyear national study.

  1. January to March 2027: Listen, through community listening sessions and leader conversations.
  2. March to April: survey design, translation and testing.
  3. May to June: fieldwork, timed to the national study.
  4. July to September: analysis and reporting.
  5. October: public release and community findings sessions.
  6. October to December: strategy workshops and guidance.
  7. November to December: focus studies on immigrant justice and on young Minneapolis.

How the work is shared

The Minneapolis Foundation

  • Hosts the Center and sets its priorities with the community advisory circle
  • Convenes residents, partners and leaders for Listen and Act
  • Brings its relationships with community organizations across the city
  • Leads the public release and the civic conversation that follows

Gallup

  • Designs and fields the survey, including sampling, translation and weighting
  • Benchmarks Minneapolis against the national Democracy for All baseline
  • Analyzes and reports findings, community by community
  • Facilitates findings sessions and strategy workshops with the data
  • Extends the reach of findings through Gallup.com and the media

Community partners

  • Co-host listening sessions in their neighborhoods and languages
  • Help residents know the survey is coming and how their answers are protected
  • Review findings and shape how they return to their communities

Why Gallup

Gallup conducts the Democracy for All Project, the most extensive annual study of how Americans experience democracy, with the Charles F. Kettering Foundation, and shared its Minnesota findings at the Foundation’s 2026 Minnesota Meeting. The Center would carry that work to the city level, with the same questions and the same web and mail design built to reach adults whom online-only research misses.

For 90 years, Gallup has measured what people think and experience, and reported it independently. The Center would apply that standard to a single city, and to every community within it.

  • 23,683U.S. adults in the 2026 national baseline
  • 5years planned for the national study, giving the city a running comparison
  • 90years of public opinion research

Minneapolis has already shown what its residents will do for one another. The Center for Civic Health would help the city see, year after year, how its democracy is working for all of them, and what to do next.
